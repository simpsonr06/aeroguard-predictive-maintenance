## 1. Aviation Domain & Regulatory Disclaimers

### 1.1 Non-Avionics Operational Scope
AeroSentinel is strictly a **ground-side decision-support analytics pipeline** intended for offline engineering review at scheduled maintenance depots.
* **NOT a Flight-Control System:** AeroSentinel does NOT interact with, interface with, or issue commands to Full Authority Digital Engine Control (FADEC), Engine Indicating and Crew Alerting Systems (EICAS), or onboard flight avionics.
* **No Certification Claims:** This implementation has NOT undergone certification under FAA/EASA guidelines, including RTCA DO-178C (Software Considerations in Airborne Systems), RTCA DO-254 (Design Assurance for Airborne Electronic Hardware), or RTCA DO-326A / ED-202A (Airworthiness Security Process).
* **Advisory Outputs Only:** All Remaining Useful Life (RUL) estimates, risk classifications, and maintenance alerts generated by this system are advisory recommendations requiring manual verification by certified Powerplant & Airframe (A&P) maintenance technicians.

## 2. Dataset & Experimental Limitations

### 2.1 Synthetic Engine Simulation (NASA C-MAPSS)
* The C-MAPSS simulation environment models thermo-aerodynamic engine physics via simplified differential equation systems.
* FD001 evaluates a single steady-state operational condition ($1.00\text{ Mach}$, sea-level ambient conditions) and a single simulated degradation mode (High-Pressure Compressor wear). Multi-condition datasets (FD002, FD004) introduce complex flight envelope transitions not evaluated in this initial study.
* Sensor noise in C-MAPSS is simulated via additive Gaussian distributions, which may not capture non-linear electro-magnetic interference (EMI), bursty packet loss, or transient avionics bus dropouts.

### 2.2 Synthetic Cyberattack Ground-Truth
* NASA C-MAPSS does not provide real-world cyberattack logs or electronic warfare telemetry.
* All sensor manipulations (Scenarios 1 through 6) are synthetically constructed deterministic perturbations designed to test mathematical sensitivity and drift detector thresholds.
* Demonstrating that a synthetic sensor perturbation evades a statistical detector does NOT prove that an adversary can trivially inject that specific perturbation into an operational ARINC 429, AFDX, or ACARS datalink.

## 3. Threat Model & Control Limitations

### 3.1 Drift Detection is Not Malice Attribution
* Statistical drift detectors (Two-Sample KS test and Population Stability Index) measure mathematical divergence between empirical probability distributions.
* Drift detection cannot determine the *root cause* of a distributional shift. Severe mechanical fouling, turbine blade tip erosion, volcanic ash ingestion, or sensor calibration loss will trigger identical statistical alarms to malicious sensor spoofing.
* Drift alarms indicate data unreliability for maintenance decision-making, not forensic certainty of hostile cyber infiltration.

### 3.2 Cryptographic Authentication Scope
* The Ed25519 signature mechanism guarantees **authenticity and tamper-evidence** of model artifacts and lineage manifests.
* Signature verification does NOT guarantee that the underlying model architecture is optimal, fair, or robust against out-of-distribution physical telemetry.
* If the private signing key ($sk_{\text{ed25519}}$) is compromised or leaked, an adversary can sign arbitrary malicious artifacts. Robust key management (HSM, KMS) is assumed for production environments.

### 3.3 Ephemeral State & In-Memory Observability
* Drift evaluation buffers and request latency logs currently reside in-memory for microservice efficiency.
* In the absence of a distributed time-series database (e.g., TimescaleDB, InfluxDB), restart of the containerized microservice resets historical sliding windows.
